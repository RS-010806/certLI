\small
\textbf{What I ran.} I implemented CertLI end to end and tested it on two ColBERT models (ColBERTv2 and AnswerAI-ColBERT-small) and two BEIR corpora (SciFact and NFCorpus). The pipeline has five parts: the corpus-mean shift (Lemma 1), low-rank-plus-sparse compression, interval bounds on MaxSim (Lemma 2), exact rescoring in the order of the bounds, and a calibrated variant using Learn-then-Test \cite{3}. All results are measured against exact MaxSim over the whole corpus, so nothing depends on a candidate generator.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Findings.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{The math holds on real models.} Subtracting the corpus mean left every ranking unchanged, and the bounds were never violated in 50.3M (query, document) checks.
\item \textbf{Deterministic certificates are too costly at useful compression.} With the index compressed to about one third of its size, proving the exact top-10 still requires scoring 79\% to 95\% of the corpus for ColBERTv2 and all of it for AnswerAI-small (Figure~\ref{fig:reads}, grey).
\item \textbf{The reason is clear in the data.} The coding error points in a nearly random direction relative to the query, so it is typically 5\% of its worst-case bound, while the 10th and 11th exact scores are separated by only 0.08 (ColBERTv2) or less than 0.01 (AnswerAI).
\item \textbf{Calibrated certificates work.} Shrinking the same intervals by a factor chosen with Learn-then-Test (at most 5\% of queries with a wrong top-10, with 90\% confidence) cuts the cost to 1.4\% of the corpus on ColBERTv2/SciFact, with 0.7\% of held-out queries wrong (Figure~\ref{fig:reads}, blue). A fixed rerank depth calibrated the same way is equally cheap, so the gain comes from calibration rather than from adapting to each query; a perfect stopping rule would need 3 to 7 times fewer documents, which is the room left for improvement.
\item \textbf{A finding relevant to EigenLI \cite{2}.} AnswerAI-small tokens share one dominant direction. Removing it is exactly safe for MaxSim but not for spectral scores: removing it from documents only drops an EigenLI-style score from 68.0 to 1.6 nDCG@10.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}

\section{Setup}
\textbf{Models and data.} ColBERTv2 \cite{6} (128-dimensional tokens) and AnswerAI-ColBERT-small-v1 (96 dimensions), encoded with PyLate using each model's standard settings (queries expanded to 32 tokens, documents cut at 300 tokens). Corpora: BEIR SciFact (5,183 documents, 300 test queries) and NFCorpus (3,633 documents, 323 test queries). Ranking quality is nDCG@10 against exact MaxSim, $C(Q,D)=\sum_i\max_j\langle q_i,d_j\rangle$, computed over the whole corpus.

\textbf{Compression.} After subtracting the corpus mean $\mu$, each document is stored in its own eigenbasis of at most 32 directions. Tokens that this basis represents poorly (residual above $\tau$) are kept exactly; the rest are grouped into small clusters (radius at most $\varepsilon$), each stored with its own error radii. Both $\tau$ and $\varepsilon$ are relative to the typical token norm, and sizes are reported as a share of the uncompressed fp16 index. For comparison, I certify two simpler codes with the same bounds: POOL, which clusters tokens without a subspace, and a PLAID-style residual code \cite{8}.

\textbf{Cost.} Every document gets an interval $[L_D,U_D]$ that is guaranteed to contain its exact score. Documents are rescored exactly in decreasing order of $U_D$ until the next upper bound falls below the current 10th best score, which reads the fewest documents any correct method can read with these intervals \cite{4}. I report this number as a share of the corpus. All runs used 2 CPU cores and no GPU.

\section{Takeaways and next steps}
\textbf{What worked.} Both lemmas hold and are cheap to apply. The low-rank-plus-sparse structure is real and saves about 30\% of memory over plain clustering at the same certification cost. Calibrated certificates control the error while scoring 1.4\% to 12.6\% of the corpus in three of the four settings.

\textbf{What did not.} Deterministic certificates still score most of the corpus at useful compression, because worst-case bounds ignore that the coding error is nearly random in direction and that top-10 margins are tiny. Adapting the stopping point to each query does not yet beat a calibrated fixed depth.

\textbf{Next steps.} (1) Apply a random rotation or dither \cite{5} before compression, so that the near-isotropy observed here becomes a guarantee; the bound can then rely on concentration instead of the worst case and give a high-probability certificate without calibration data. (2) Learn the stopping rule from score margins and density, aiming at the 3 to 7 times headroom shown by the oracle. (3) Scale to MS MARCO and LoTTE with PLAID candidate generation on GPU, certifying PLAID's own compressed codes.

\textbf{Scope.} Small corpora (3.6k and 5.2k documents), CPU only, exact scoring of the full corpus as ground truth, and 150 to 162 calibration queries per split. EigenLI is my re-implementation, not the authors' code.
